🕯️ Welcome & First Cup of Wine

Moto lights the candles and raises a glass.

Welcome, everyone. Look around this table for a moment. Look at the faces. Some of us have been doing this together for years. Some of us are here for the first time. We have a six-year-old and we have people who remember when the world looked very different. That range — that span of ages and lives and stories — is itself a kind of miracle.

Tonight we tell an old story about people who were enslaved and then got free. We have been telling this same story for thousands of years — parent to child, grandparent to grandchild, stranger to stranger at tables just like this one — because the story has never stopped being true. People are still enslaved. People are still fleeing. People are still being told they don’t belong in their own bodies, their own lands, their own forests, their own families. The machinery of cruelty keeps running, and it counts on us not paying attention.

So we gather here, in this specific room, with these specific people, and we do the opposite of not paying attention. We light candles. We pour wine. We eat matzah that tastes like cardboard — because nothing says ‘celebration of freedom’ like a cracker with no yeast and no flavor and the structural integrity of a roofing tile — and we remember. We remember not because it’s comfortable, but because forgetting is how injustice wins.

This year, especially, we refuse to look away. From any of it. And we hold onto each other while we look.

Let’s begin with the first cup of wine — or grape juice, no judgment. The first cup is for the promise that was made: I will bring you out.

Everyone raises their glass.

Everyone: Blessed are You, Source of all life, who creates the fruit of the vine.

Drink the first cup.

Next: Daisy — Karpas

🌿 Karpas — Greens & Salt Water

We dip a green vegetable in salt water. This is one of the simplest rituals of the night, and one of the most honest.

The green is for spring — for things growing, for life pushing up through soil that was frozen just weeks ago. The earth does this every year without being asked. It renews itself even when the news is unbearable, even when the rivers are poisoned, even when we have failed it in a hundred ways. The crocuses come up anyway. The parsley grows. Spring does not wait for us to deserve it.

The salt water is for tears. For the tears of enslaved people who built someone else’s monuments. For the tears of parents who could not protect their children. For the tears we have cried this year — and there is no shortage. There never is.

So we hold both at once, in the same hand, in the same bite: the green and the salt. The renewal and the grief. Joy does not cancel out sorrow, and sorrow does not cancel out joy. They exist together, braided, inseparable. That is what it means to be alive and paying attention.

Everyone dips their greens in salt water and eats.

Next: Moto — Yachatz

Yachatz — Breaking the Matzah

Moto breaks the middle matzah in two.

This is the bread of affliction, the bread our ancestors ate in the land of Egypt. We hold it up so everyone can see it, and then we break it in two.

Think about that for a moment. We begin this seder not with something whole, but with something broken. That’s not an accident. It’s a statement: we start from brokenness. We acknowledge that the world is not complete, that we are not complete, that something essential is still missing. The rabbis understood that you cannot begin the work of liberation by pretending everything is fine.

One half stays here on the table — visible, present, the bread of our poverty. The other half gets wrapped up and hidden. That hidden piece is called the afikomen, and we cannot finish the seder without it. It represents what is not yet found, what is not yet redeemed, what is still waiting to be discovered. The meal cannot end until someone goes looking for it.

The practical implication is that a certain six-year-old is about to have some serious negotiating leverage later tonight. Ollie, take notes.

Moto hides the afikomen. Ollie, pay attention. Or don’t — your call.

⛓️ The Story — We Were Slaves

We were slaves in Egypt. That is where the story begins — not with a hero, not with a miracle, but with suffering. With people who woke up every morning to labor they did not choose, who built cities they would never live in, who watched their children be taken from them by a ruler who feared what he could not control.

Pharaoh was not unusual in his cruelty. He was a powerful man who looked at a population of people who were different from him — who spoke differently, worshipped differently, lived differently — and he saw a threat. Not because they were dangerous, but because there were a lot of them and they were growing. Throughout history, that combination has made powerful people do terrible things. It still does. The playbook has not changed: first you dehumanize, then you exploit, then you convince everyone else it was necessary.

The Israelites cried out. And the story tells us that God heard them. However you understand God — as a presence, as a force, as a metaphor for the human conscience that eventually cannot tolerate injustice — something heard them.

And then God sent Moses. Moses, who did not want the job. Who stammered. Who had fled Egypt years earlier and was living a quiet life tending sheep in the desert. When God spoke to him from the burning bush and said, ‘Go back and free my people,’ Moses said, essentially, ‘Can you send someone else?’ There is something deeply important in the fact that the liberator of the Jewish people was a reluctant leader. He did not seek power. He did not want to be in charge. He just happened to be the one who could not say no when the call came.

Moses went to Pharaoh and said, ‘Let my people go.’ Pharaoh said no. Moses came back. Pharaoh said no again. This happened over and over, because people with too much power almost always say no. They say no the first time and the second time and the ninth time, because saying yes would mean giving up something they believe is theirs by right. Pharaoh’s stubbornness is not ancient history. It is the stubbornness of every system that refuses to release its grip, every institution that chooses its own preservation over someone else’s freedom.

We tell this story not because it ended. We tell it because it hasn’t.

Next: Monica — Plagues and Freedom

🌊 The Story — Plagues and Freedom

So there were plagues. Ten of them. Water turned to blood. Frogs swarmed the land. Lice. Flies. Disease swept through the livestock. Boils erupted on skin. Hail rained down and shattered everything it touched. Locusts devoured what was left. Darkness — not the ordinary darkness of night, but a darkness so thick you could feel it, a darkness that pressed against you and made it impossible to see the person next to you.

And then the last plague, the worst one, the one that finally broke Pharaoh: the death of the firstborn.

These plagues were terrible. They were terrible for the Egyptians who had nothing to do with Pharaoh’s decisions, for the children who had never enslaved anyone, for the animals who were just animals. Liberation came at an enormous cost, and we do not celebrate that cost. Later tonight, we will spill drops of wine — diminishing our own joy — because our freedom was purchased with someone else’s suffering. Every liberation is. That is one of the hardest truths of this story.

After the tenth plague, Pharaoh finally said go. And the people went. They went so fast that the bread didn’t rise — hence the matzah. Imagine that moment: hundreds of thousands of people grabbing what they could carry, wrapping dough around their bodies, pulling children by the hand, walking out of the only life they had ever known. They were terrified. They were exhilarated. They had no plan. They just knew they had to move.

And then they reached the sea. Water in front of them, Pharaoh’s army behind them, and nowhere to go. The midrash says that the sea did not part until one person — Nachshon — walked in up to his nostrils. Only then did the waters split. Faith is not the absence of fear. It is walking into the water anyway.

They crossed. They were free. And then came the hard part, which is always the hard part, the part that takes longer than the crossing and is less dramatic but infinitely more important: figuring out what to do with freedom. How to build something. How to be a people. How not to replicate the cruelty you just escaped. We are still working on that.

Next: Zoe — The Ten Plagues

🍷 The Ten Plagues

As we name each plague, we spill a drop of wine from our cups. This is one of the most important gestures of the seder. Our cup of joy cannot be full when others have suffered for our sake. Even when those others opposed us. Even when those others were our oppressors. Their suffering diminishes our celebration, because if we could rejoice fully in someone else’s pain, we would have learned nothing from being enslaved ourselves.

If that principle held up more often in the world — if nations and leaders could grieve the cost of their victories, if the powerful could see the humanity of those they defeat — we would be in very different shape.

Remove a drop of wine for each plague.

  • Blood
  • Frogs
  • Lice
  • Flies
  • Cattle disease
  • Boils
  • Hail
  • Locusts
  • Darkness
  • Death of the firstborn

Those were the ancient plagues. But plague is not only ancient. We might name some modern ones — not to spill wine for, but to say out loud, because naming a thing is the first step toward refusing to accept it.

The destruction of forests and wild places — entire ecosystems erased so someone can profit from the land. Children bombed in their homes and schools while the world debates whether to call it what it is. People denied medical care because of who they are or who they love. Wars that serve no one who fights in them, only those who profit from the fighting. The criminalization of poverty. The weaponization of borders against the desperate. The slow strangling of democracy by people who wrap themselves in its flag. The loneliness of a culture that has replaced connection with consumption.

We cannot spill enough wine for all of this. We cannot fix all of it tonight. But we can name it, here, together, and let the naming be a kind of refusal — a refusal to pretend that what is happening is normal, or acceptable, or someone else’s problem. We see it. We say it. And tomorrow, or next week, or whenever we can, we do something about it.

Next: Tai — Dayenu

🎵 Dayenu — It Would Have Been Enough

Dayenu means ‘it would have been enough.’ It is a song about gratitude, but more than that, it is a practice — a discipline of noticing what you have before reaching for what you don’t.

The traditional version goes like this: If God had only taken us out of Egypt but not split the sea — dayenu, it would have been enough. If God had only split the sea but not led us through the desert — dayenu. If God had only led us through the desert but not given us the Torah — dayenu. Each gift is treated as if it were the whole gift. Each step is treated as if it were the destination.

This is a radical idea in a world that always wants more, that measures success by accumulation, that treats ‘enough’ as a failure of ambition. Dayenu says: stop. Look at what is here. It is enough. You are enough.

We might add our own. If we had only found these people to share a table with tonight — dayenu. If we had only made it through another year with the people we love still here — dayenu. If we had only managed to keep showing up, keep caring, keep trying, in a year that made all of that very hard — dayenu. It would have been enough. And yet here we are, with all of this.

Fair warning: this song is extremely catchy. It will lodge itself in your brain like a piece of afikomen between your teeth. You will be humming it while doing dishes later. You will be humming it next Tuesday. This is the price of joy.

Everyone sings. Even if you don’t know the tune, just follow along.

Everyone: Da-da-yenu, da-da-yenu, da-da-yenu, dayenu dayenu!

Everyone: Da-da-yenu, da-da-yenu, da-da-yenu, dayenu dayenu!

Next: Ryan — The Three Symbols

🥘 The Three Symbols

Rabban Gamliel — a rabbi, not a character from Lord of the Rings, though the name does have that energy — said you haven’t fulfilled your duty at the seder unless you’ve discussed three things. Just three. The whole seder, all the wine, all the singing, and it comes down to these three objects on the table. Let’s give them their moment.

Pesach — the lamb bone. It represents the sacrifice our ancestors made on that last night in Egypt, when they marked their doorposts with blood so the angel of death would pass over their homes. It is a reminder that freedom required risk. They had to commit to leaving before they knew they could. The bone sits on the plate looking dramatic and slightly unsettling, which is the point. Freedom is dramatic. Freedom is slightly unsettling. It asks you to give something up before it gives you anything back.

Matzah — the flat bread. The bread of haste. Our ancestors fled in such a hurry they couldn’t wait for dough to rise. But the rabbis also call it the bread of humility — because it doesn’t puff itself up, doesn’t pretend to be more than it is. It is flour and water and that’s it. A cracker that knows its place. In a world of people who inflate themselves to seem bigger and more important, matzah is admirably honest about what it is. Dry. Flat. Crumbly. Getting everywhere. But real.

Maror — the bitter herb. Horseradish, usually. It represents the bitterness of slavery — the daily grinding misery of a life you did not choose and cannot escape. Eat enough of it and you will understand suffering on a very personal, sinus-clearing level. Your eyes will water. That’s the point. The story is not supposed to be comfortable.

Together, these three things tell the whole arc: sacrifice, humility, and bitterness. You cannot understand freedom without knowing what it cost. You cannot appreciate what you have without remembering what it felt like to have nothing. And you cannot eat this much horseradish without needing a moment to recover.

Everyone raises their glass for the second cup.

Everyone: Blessed are You, Source of all life, who creates the fruit of the vine.

Drink the second cup.

Next: Brett — Matzah, Maror & Hillel Sandwich

🥪 Matzah, Maror & the Hillel Sandwich

Alright, now we eat the ritual foods. We’ve talked about them, we’ve admired them on the plate, we’ve discussed their deep symbolic meaning. Time to actually put them in our mouths. First up: the matzah.

Take a piece and hold it for a second. This is the bread of affliction and the bread of freedom, both at once. The same cracker. It was all our ancestors had, and it was enough to carry them out of slavery. Sometimes the simplest, most stripped-down thing is what saves you.

Everyone: Blessed are You, Source of all life, who brings forth bread from the earth.

Everyone eats a piece of matzah.

Now the bitter herb. Take a piece of maror — that’s the horseradish — and dip it in the charoset. The charoset is the sweet brown paste made from apples and nuts and wine. It represents the mortar the slaves used to build Pharaoh’s cities. Even in something that represents forced labor, there is sweetness. Even in the memory of suffering, there is something that nourishes. Dip the bitter into the sweet. Eat them together.

Everyone eats maror with charoset.

And now the main event: the Hillel sandwich. Take two pieces of matzah and put maror and charoset between them. This was Hillel’s innovation — Rabbi Hillel, who lived two thousand years ago and who understood something profound about the human experience: you cannot separate the bitter from the sweet. They come together. They are eaten together. They are lived together.

Think about the people you love most. Think about the moments of your life that meant the most. They are inseparable from loss, from worry, from the knowledge that nothing lasts. The sandwich is a metaphor you can eat: suffering and sweetness, held between two pieces of the humblest bread on earth. Two thousand years old, and still the best sandwich recipe involving zero leavened bread and one hundred percent existential wisdom.

Everyone eats the Hillel sandwich.

🚪 After the Meal — Afikomen & Elijah’s Cup

Alright. The meal is done. The plates are wrecked. Someone definitely spilled something. Now it’s time for the part Ollie has been waiting for all night.

The afikomen — that hidden half of matzah — needs to be found. The seder cannot end without it. Think about what that means: we broke something at the beginning of the evening, and now we cannot be complete until the missing piece is returned. The search for the afikomen is a search for wholeness. It is the insistence that we do not leave things broken if we can help it, that we go looking for what is lost, that we do not finish the story until every piece is accounted for.

Ollie — and anyone who helped — go find it. Negotiations will follow. This is tradition.

The afikomen is found. Negotiations ensue. Dessert matzah is eaten by all.

Now we fill Elijah’s cup. Elijah is the prophet who, according to tradition, visits every seder table in the world tonight. He is the herald of redemption — the one who will announce that the world has finally, fully changed. That justice has arrived. That the work is done.

We open the door for him now. And here is the thing about opening the door: some years it feels like a joyful gesture, full of confidence that better days are coming. Other years — and this may be one of them — it feels like a heavier thing. Like standing at the threshold between hope and despair and choosing, deliberately, to face the direction of hope. Not because you are sure. Not because the evidence supports it. But because hope is a decision, not a feeling, and tonight we make that decision together.

Someone opens the door. A moment of silence. Feel the night air. Listen for what might be coming.

Next: Daisy — Miriam’s Cup

💧 Miriam’s Cup

Before we close, we honor Miriam.

Miriam was Moses’s older sister. When Moses was a baby, placed in a basket in the Nile because Pharaoh had ordered the death of every Hebrew boy, it was Miriam who stood on the riverbank and watched. She was a child herself, but she stood there, refusing to look away, making sure her brother survived. When Pharaoh’s daughter found the basket, it was Miriam who stepped forward and said, ‘I know a woman who can nurse this baby’ — and brought Moses back to their own mother. She was brave and quick and she saved his life with nothing but her wits and her willingness to speak up.

Years later, after the sea crossing, when everyone was stunned and trembling on the far shore, it was Miriam who picked up a tambourine and started singing. She led the people in dance. She gave them a way to hold the enormity of what had just happened. Sometimes survival needs a song more than it needs a speech.

The tradition of Miriam’s Cup is newer than most of what we do tonight. It was created to honor the women whose work has always kept communities alive — the ones who watched, who nursed, who sang, who organized, who fed, who held things together when everything was falling apart. Their names are not always in the official story. They did the work anyway.

We fill Miriam’s cup with water, not wine. Water because Miriam was associated with a miraculous well that traveled with the Israelites through the desert, providing water wherever they camped. Water because it is the most basic, essential, life-sustaining thing. Water because the work of sustaining a community is not glamorous. It is daily. It is quiet. It is absolutely necessary.

Tonight we see you. All of you — the ones whose names we know and the ones whose names were never written down. We see you, and we are grateful.

Everyone raises Miriam’s cup of water.

Next: Everyone — The Third Cup & Closing

🌅 The Third Cup & Closing

Everyone raises their glass for the third cup.

Everyone: Blessed are You, Source of all life, who creates the fruit of the vine.

Drink the third cup.

That’s our seder. We made it through — the candles, the questions, the plagues, the songs, the sandwiches, the negotiations over a hidden cracker. The matzah was dry. The horseradish cleared some sinuses. The wine flowed. A door was opened for someone who may or may not have visited.

But here is what actually happened tonight: we sat together. We, these specific people, in this specific room, told a story that has been told for three thousand years and made it ours. We named the suffering of the past and the suffering of the present, and we did not look away. We sang. We laughed. We ate a meal together, which is one of the oldest and most sacred things human beings can do.

Next year, may the world have less to grieve. May the wars end. May the forests still stand. May the people who are fleeing tonight find safety. May the people who are imprisoned for who they are be free. May the children who are afraid right now find themselves, by next Passover, safe and bored and complaining about matzah like every kid should get to do.

Next year, may we be together again — all of us, and maybe a few more. May this table grow. May we still be the kind of people who light candles and ask questions and refuse to be comfortable when comfort requires looking away.

And if next year is hard — and it may be — may we remember this night. May we remember that we were not alone. That we had wine and salt water and bad crackers and each other, and that it was enough. Dayenu. It was enough.

Everyone: Next year in Jerusalem! Next year, may all people be free!

The seder is over. Refill your wine. You’ve earned it.

Chag Pesach Sameach! 🍷
